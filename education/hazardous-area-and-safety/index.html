Field Pocket Guide

Hazardous area, IS barriers and safety loops

This page covers the two problems that share a panel in a classified area and get confused with each other: keeping ignition energy out of the field, and keeping a trip function working when it is asked. Barrier grounding, conduit seals, area classification, and SIL verification all land on the same drawing and usually on the same technician.

The governing principle for all of it: a marking on a nameplate is a permission for a specific installation under specific conditions, not a property the device carries into your loop. Intrinsic safety is assessed as a system, not as a device in isolation. SIL is a property of the loop, not of the boxes in it. Get the installed condition right and the marking becomes true; get it wrong and the certificate is a piece of paper in a file.

Do intrinsic safety barriers need their own dedicated IS ground, and what resistance does it have to be?

Zener barriers need a dedicated IS ground of less than 1.0 Ω back to the grounding electrode system; galvanic isolators do not.

A zener barrier does three things at once: zener diodes clamp voltage to ground, a series resistor limits current, and a fuse protects the diodes from the clamped current. All three depend on a low-impedance path to earth. If that path opens or goes high, the clamp has nowhere to send fault current and the barrier's entity parameters stop describing the circuit. The working number is less than 1.0 Ω from the barrier ground bus to the grounding electrode system.

Know where that number comes from, because it is not NEC text. NEC 504.50 requires associated apparatus to be grounded and 504.60 covers bonding, but neither sets a resistance limit. The 1 Ω figure and a 12 AWG (3.3 mm²) minimum grounding conductor come from ANSI/ISA-RP12.06.01 and from the barrier manufacturer's control drawing, which is the document the installation is actually judged against. IEC 60079-14 sets a minimum cross-section of 4 mm² for an intrinsically safe earth conductor. Those are not the same requirement — 12 AWG is 3.31 mm², and a job worked to IEC needs the larger conductor. Two conductors are commonly run in parallel so that removing one does not open the path.

Measure it with a 4-wire low-resistance ohmmeter, not a handheld DMM. Test lead and probe contact resistance by itself is commonly 0.1 Ω to 0.5 Ω, a large fraction of the budget before you reach the bus. Do not assume the DIN rail carries the IS ground unless the control drawing says the rail assembly is listed for that duty.

Galvanic isolators work differently. They limit energy through transformer or optical isolation rather than a shunt to ground, so no dedicated IS ground is required. That is the main reason isolators have displaced zener barriers on new work. The exception: some isolating modules still call for an earth connection on their control drawing, usually a screen or functional-earth terminal, so read the drawing rather than assuming the terminal is decorative. Two other Article 504 items get missed on the same panel:

  • 504.30 separation — IS and non-IS conductors kept at least 50 mm (2 in.) apart, or separated by a grounded metal partition or an approved insulating partition. The 2 in. is one permitted method, not the only one.
  • 504.80 identification — labels on the raceways, cable trays and boxes are what the code requires. Light blue is permitted as a color code for IS conductors where no other conductors in the installation are light blue. It is not required, and light blue wire with no labels does not satisfy 504.80.

Why does my SIL verification come out as SIL 2 when every device in the loop is SIL 3 certified?

Because SIL belongs to the loop and PFDavg adds across every element, so the largest single contributor sets the result — and it is almost always the final element.

In low demand mode the bands are SIL 1: PFDavg 10-2 to 10-1 (risk reduction factor 10 to 100), SIL 2: 10-3 to 10-2 (100 to 1,000), and SIL 3: 10-4 to 10-3 (1,000 to 10,000). "Certified to SIL 3" means a device is capable of being used in a SIL 3 function under the conditions in its safety manual. It does not mean it delivers a SIL 3 PFD in your configuration. Run the arithmetic on a typical 1oo1 loop proof tested once a year. The failure rates below are generic illustrative values — replace every one of them with the numbers in your own devices' safety manuals before you quote a result:

  • Transmitter, λDU = 1 × 10-7/h (100 FIT): 1E-07 × 8,760 / 2 = 4.4 × 10-4
  • Certified logic solver: 1 × 10-5
  • Valve, actuator and solenoid, λDU = 5 × 10-7/h, 90% proof test coverage, 10-year mission: (0.9 × 5E-07 × 8,760 / 2) + (0.1 × 5E-07 × 87,600 / 2) = 2.0E-03 + 2.2E-03 = 4.2 × 10-3

Loop total 4.6 × 10-3, RRF 217. That is SIL 2, and the final element is 90% of it. Nothing was wrong with the transmitter.

Three separate barriers have to clear and the lowest one wins. The PFDavg band is the arithmetic above. The architectural constraints are separate: IEC 61511-1 Table 6 requires a minimum hardware fault tolerance of 1 at SIL 3, so a 1oo1 architecture cannot claim SIL 3 unless the HFT reduction allowance in clause 11.4 is justified — and that allowance carries conditions, including selection based on prior use and adjustment limited to protected process-related parameters. Systematic capability is the third and it is a true weakest-link cap: one element with systematic capability SC 2 holds the whole function at SIL 2 no matter how good the PFD arithmetic is.

Before blaming the software, check these: proof test coverage entered as 100% when the procedure never strokes the valve to its seat; MTTR entered as 8 hours when the site's real repair time is 72; the solenoid, interposing relay, instrument air supply or trip amplifier left out of the model entirely; generic failure data used in place of the numbers in the device's own safety manual.

Does using a SIL 3 certified logic solver mean my whole safety loop is SIL 3?

No — the logic solver is typically only 10% to 15% of the loop's PFD budget, and it can neither cause nor fix a shortfall that lives in the sensor or the final element.

A safety instrumented function is a series arrangement and the PFDs add. A certified logic solver usually contributes 1 × 10-5 to 1 × 10-4, one to two orders of magnitude below the SIL 3 ceiling of 1 × 10-3. The rule-of-thumb budget split — and it is a rule of thumb, not a requirement in either standard — is sensor about 35%, logic solver 10% to 15%, final element about 50%. If the valve subsystem is sitting at 4 × 10-3, swapping a SIL 2 logic solver for a SIL 3 logic solver moves the loop total by less than 1 × 10-4, about two percent. The shortfall does not live there.

The certificate is also conditional, and the conditions are in the safety manual, not on the certificate. Typical ones: a stated proof test interval and a specific proof test procedure, a maximum MTTR, an ambient temperature limit, which I/O modules are covered, whether outputs are de-energize-to-trip, and whether output line monitoring is enabled. Violate one and the certified failure rates no longer describe your installation. Systematic capability is the other cap — every element must be at or above the target, so an SC 2 sensor with an SC 3 logic solver gives an SC 2 function.

The application program is yours, not the vendor's. IEC 61511-1 clause 12 covers application program development, and a certified logic solver running unverified logic is not a certified safety function. Cause-and-effect verification, bypass management, and testing after change are part of the claim.

What happens to my SIL rating if I extend the proof test interval from 1 year to 3 years?

The periodically tested part of the undetected dangerous contribution triples, which typically halves your risk reduction factor and can cost you the SIL band.

For a 1oo1 element with perfect testing, PFDavg ≈ λDU × TI / 2. Going from 8,760 h to 26,280 h multiplies that term by three. On the loop above, the one-year total was 4.6 × 10-3 at RRF 217. At three years the transmitter goes to 1.3 × 10-3, the valve subsystem to 8.1 × 10-3, and the loop totals 9.4 × 10-3 at RRF 106. That is still inside SIL 2, but the SIL 2 limit is 1 × 10-2, so the function is 6% away from being a SIL 1 loop with a SIL 2 label on the drawing.

Imperfect proof testing changes the shape of that. With coverage below 100% the model is PFDavg ≈ PTC × λDU × TI/2 + (1 − PTC) × λDU × LT/2, where LT is mission time. Only the first term triples. The second term is set by mission time and does not move with your interval at all. Once the second term dominates, stretching the interval adds risk without buying much, and shortening it does not help much either — at that point the answer is overhaul or replacement, not more testing.

Two credits that are routinely taken and should not be: a partial stroke test is not a proof test — typical PST coverage is 60% to 70% of a valve's dangerous undetected failures, while a full stroke to seat with a leakage check is more like 90% or better. And transmitter self-diagnostics do not test the impulse line, the manifold, or the tap; a plugged tap is a dangerous undetected failure that only an applied-pressure proof test finds.

Changing the interval is not a maintenance decision. It changes the safety requirements specification, it requires the SIL verification to be re-run, and under OSHA PSM it is a management of change item under 29 CFR 1910.119(l). Editing the frequency field in the CMMS accomplishes none of that.

What is the difference between intrinsically safe and explosion proof?

Intrinsic safety prevents ignition by limiting the energy in the field circuit; explosion proof accepts that ignition may occur inside the enclosure and contains it.

Intrinsic safety caps voltage, current and stored energy so the worst-case spark or surface temperature cannot ignite the gas group, under normal operation and under specified faults. Ex ia is safe with two countable faults and is accepted in Zone 0; Ex ib tolerates one fault and is Zone 1; Ex ic is Zone 2 with no fault applied. That ia/ib/ic split is Zone-system marking — under the Division system the governing text is NEC Article 504 and the apparatus is listed for Class I Division 1 or Division 2. Explosion proof, called flameproof (Ex d) outside North America, uses a heavy enclosure with machined flame paths that cool escaping combustion products below the ignition temperature of the surrounding atmosphere.

The maintenance difference is the one that matters at the panel. An IS loop can be worked live in the classified area — landing a lead, connecting a communicator — because the protection method itself does not require a hot work permit. Two conditions attach. The test instrument has to be certified for the area or covered by the control drawing; an ordinary DMM or communicator is not IS apparatus, and connecting one defeats the certification for as long as it is on the loop. And site procedure can still require a permit regardless of what the method allows. An Ex d enclosure is only safe closed and correctly assembled: opening it live needs a gas test and a permit, and the flame path is defeated by a missing bolt, a painted or corroded mating surface, or fewer than five threads fully engaged at a conduit entry (NEC 500.8(E)); factory-threaded NPT entries are permitted at 4-1/2 threads.

IS also costs you power. A common 28 V, 300 Ω zener barrier has entity parameters around Uo 28 V, Io 93 mA, Po 0.65 W. Those are worst-case maximums, not simultaneous output — Po = Uo × Io / 4. In normal operation the number that bites is the series resistance: 300 Ω at 22 mA is 6.6 V gone before the transmitter sees anything, on top of the drop across a 250 Ω sense resistor. That budget runs a 4-20 mA transmitter and will not run a heater, a motor, or most solenoids. That is why sensors are usually IS and final elements are usually Ex d on the same skid.

Intrinsic safety is assessed as a system, so both ends and the cable have to be checked against the control drawing:

  • Voc (Uo) ≤ Vmax (Ui)
  • Isc (Io) ≤ Imax (Ii)
  • Po ≤ Pi
  • Ca (Co) ≥ Ci + Ccable
  • La (Lo) ≥ Li + Lcable

Where cable data is unknown, IEC 60079-14 permits 200 pF/m and 1 µH/m as default values. On a 300 m run that is 60 nF, enough to exceed Ca on some barriers with no device connected at all. Neither method is safer than the other. They fail in different ways, and the failure mode should drive the choice.

Do I still need a conduit seal within 18 inches of an explosion proof enclosure?

Yes in Class I Division 1, unless the enclosure is marked to say a seal is not required — NEC 501.15(A)(1) requires the seal within 450 mm (18 in.) of each entry into an explosionproof enclosure containing arcing or high-temperature parts.

The trigger is what is inside: switches, circuit breakers, fuses, relays, resistors, or anything else that can produce arcs, sparks or high temperatures. Between the seal and the enclosure, only explosionproof unions, couplings, reducers, elbows and capped elbows no larger than the trade size of the conduit are permitted. A second trigger has nothing to do with arcing — 501.15(A)(2) requires a seal within 450 mm (18 in.) at every entry of trade size 2 or larger into an enclosure housing terminals, splices or taps.

The exception people miss is printed on the nameplate. Many field instruments are marked "factory sealed," "seal not required," or equivalent, and where the enclosure is so marked no additional seal is needed at that entry. Two limits on that. A factory seal does not satisfy a boundary seal — if that entry is also where the run leaves the classified location, the boundary seal is still owed. And the marking exception carries a trade size limit in the code text, so read the section rather than assuming it covers every entry on the device. In Division 2 the rule is narrower: 501.15(B)(1) requires a seal only where conduit enters an enclosure that is required to be explosionproof, with the same 18 in. limit. If the enclosure is not required to be explosionproof, no seal is required at it.

Boundary seals are a separate requirement and get confused with the 18 in. rule constantly. A seal is required where a conduit run leaves a Division 1 location, and where a run passes from a Division 2 location into an unclassified location. Those may sit on either side of the boundary within 3.05 m (10 ft), with no coupling, box or fitting other than a listed reducer between the seal and the boundary. The Division 2 boundary seal has a real exception: unbroken metal conduit that passes completely through the Division 2 location, with no fittings within 300 mm (12 in.) of each boundary and both terminations in unclassified locations, does not have to be sealed.

Numbers for the pour itself: compound thickness at least the trade size of the fitting and never less than 16 mm (5/8 in.); conductor fill no more than 25% of the cross-sectional area of rigid metal conduit of the same trade size unless the fitting is approved for more; no splices or taps inside a sealing fitting. Pack the fiber dam first — a seal poured without a dam runs down the conduit and cures into a plug that looks finished and seals nothing. Intrinsically safe circuits fall under NEC 504.70: seals are required where the location requires them, but an IS seal does not have to be explosionproof.

What is the difference between Class I Division 2 and Zone 2?

They describe nearly the same exposure, but Division 2 is the lower half of a two-level scheme while Zone 2 is the bottom of a three-level scheme, and the gas groups, markings and permitted protection methods are not interchangeable.

The Division scheme (NEC Articles 500-504) has two levels: Division 1, where the atmosphere is present under normal operation, and Division 2, where it is present only under abnormal conditions. The Zone scheme (NEC Article 505 for gas, IEC 60079-10-1) has three: Zone 0 (continuous or long periods), Zone 1 (likely in normal operation), and Zone 2 (not likely, and short-lived if it happens). The hour bands attached to those — more than 1,000 h/yr, 10 to 1,000 h/yr, and under 10 h/yr — are common practice guidance, not NEC or IEC text; do not quote them to an AHJ as code. The mapping is Class I Division 1 ≈ Zone 0 plus Zone 1, and Class I Division 2 ≈ Zone 2. Division 2 and Zone 2 are the closest thing to a one-to-one match the two systems have.

Gas groups do not map by letter, and this is where the wrong device gets ordered. Division Group A (acetylene) and Group B (hydrogen) both map to Zone Group IIC — though hydrogen service is sometimes covered by an IIB+H₂ marking, which is hydrogen without acetylene. Group C (ethylene) maps to IIB. Group D (propane, and most general hydrocarbon service) maps to IIA. IIC is the most severe group, which is the opposite of what people expect from A being first in the alphabet.

Temperature classes share endpoints but are not identical. Both systems run T1 450 °C down to T6 85 °C. The Division system also carries subdivisions the Zone system does not have: T2A 280 °C, T2B 260 °C, T2C 230 °C, T2D 215 °C, T3A 180 °C, T3B 165 °C, T3C 160 °C, T4A 120 °C. A Division nameplate marked T3B has no Zone equivalent finer than T3.

On equipment, NEC 505.9(C) permits equipment listed for Class I Division 1 in Zone 1 or Zone 2, and equipment listed for Class I Division 2 in Zone 2, provided gas group and temperature class match. The reverse is not a blanket permission — Zone-marked equipment installed in a Division-classified area needs AHJ acceptance. The practical reason to use Zone at all is that increased safety (Ex e) and non-sparking (Ex ec / Ex nA) have no Division equivalent, so a Zone 1 area can use an Ex e terminal box where a Division 1 area would need Ex d. A single area is classified under one scheme or the other, not both, and whichever is used, the classification has to be documented and available to the people who design, install and maintain the equipment.

What is the difference between IEC 61508 and IEC 61511?

IEC 61508 is the standard a device manufacturer certifies against; IEC 61511 is the standard an owner, engineering firm and maintenance organization work to when applying those devices in a process plant.

IEC 61508 is the generic functional safety standard for electrical, electronic and programmable electronic safety-related systems, applicable to any industry, in 7 parts. It governs device design: failure rate analysis, safe failure fraction, diagnostic coverage, systematic capability, and software development rigor. A "SIL 3 certified transmitter" is certified against IEC 61508-2 for its hardware and IEC 61508-3 for its embedded software. IEC 61511 is the process sector application standard, in 3 parts, second edition 2016, and it covers the whole plant safety lifecycle: hazard and risk assessment, SIL allocation, the safety requirements specification, design and engineering, installation and commissioning, validation, operation, proof testing, management of change, and decommissioning.

In the United States, ANSI/ISA 61511-1-2018 adopts IEC 61511-1:2016; the older ANSI/ISA-84.00.01-2004 mirrored the first edition and carried a grandfather clause for existing installations. OSHA has treated ISA 84 as a recognized and generally accepted good engineering practice under the PSM standard's process safety information clause, 29 CFR 1910.119(d)(3)(ii) — the rule text does not name the standard, the RAGAGEP obligation does the work.

The link between the two is device selection. IEC 61511-1 clause 11.5 gives two routes: a device certified to IEC 61508, or a device justified by prior use with documented operating experience in comparable service. Prior use is a legitimate route and not a shortcut — it requires records of hours in service, failure history and the same operating environment. If you write the trip logic, you are in IEC 61511-1 clause 12 (application program), not IEC 61508-3, and limited variability and full variability languages carry different requirements.

What this means at the panel: nothing you buy makes you compliant. Compliance with 61511 is documents, competency records, proof test records and change control. A certificate on the transmitter is one line in that file.

When this page runs out

This page stops where field work stops. Area classification drawings are not written at a panel — if a drawing needs to be created or revised, that is a process safety engineer working from API RP 500 or API RP 505 with the AHJ. A SIL target comes out of a LOPA with operations and process engineering in the room, and no arithmetic on this page substitutes for that determination. If what is needed is a device certificate, a certificate review, or a signed functional safety assessment, that is a certification body such as TÜV or exida. And if the question is whether an existing installation complies with the code as installed, the AHJ decides, not a consultant. Bridges Industrial will say so when the answer is someone else.
